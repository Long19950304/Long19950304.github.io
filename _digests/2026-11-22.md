## 3. Customized Light for Plant Factories

ScienceNet News reports that a Chinese research team developed a plant-factory system that adjusts lighting for different crops and feeds measurements back into a closed loop. The team also combines optical filtering with photovoltaic generation and is gathering crop-growth data to iteratively optimize lighting recipes.

<p class="small text-muted">Technology &amp; research · 科学网新闻 · 2026-11-21 · <a href="https://news.sciencenet.cn/htmlnews/2026/11/575116.shtm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Original report</a></p>

<details><summary>Read the full narration</summary>

ScienceNet News, citing China News Service reporter Zhang Jun, described a research team at the Advanced Technology Institute of the University of Science and Technology of China that has studied light and crops for more than ten years. Its plant-factory system adjusts lamp intensity, spectrum, and photoperiod for different plants. A multichannel light quantum sensor measures photon numbers in individual wavelength bands, and the readings return to the control system for closed-loop adjustment, according to the report.

The team also developed a polymer optical membrane. It transmits useful red and blue light to plants while reflecting other wavelengths toward photovoltaic panels for electricity generation. The report says the membrane blocks most heat, leaving plant surfaces somewhat cooler and reducing water evaporation. These are the research team’s described characteristics, rather than independently verified performance measurements.

For tomatoes, the team says natural red-to-blue light is about four to five, while its factory setting is nine to one. The report attributes faster lycopene production and better taste to that setting. Crop-growth data collected under different recipes are also used to train a plant-growth model and iteratively refine the recipes.

The article says container factories have entered deserts, islands, and snowy highlands and have traveled aboard cargo ships, with products sold in Malaysia, Singapore, and other markets. Its forward-looking target is lower energy use and lower cost, but no timetable or quantified reduction was provided.

## 4. Inkling 2 Mini Targets Local AI

Hugging Face says Thinking Machines Lab’s Inkling 2 Mini is a multimodal, looped mixture-of-experts model designed for local AI setups. The publisher reports broad tooling support, but explicitly says its initial comparison is based on informal tests and needs more validation.

<p class="small text-muted">Technology &amp; research · Hugging Face · 2026-11-20 · <a href="https://huggingface.co/blog/inkling2mini" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Primary source</a></p>

<details><summary>Read the full narration</summary>

Hugging Face announced Inkling 2 Mini on November 20, 2026, describing it as a new model from Thinking Machines Lab designed for local AI setups. According to the publisher, it is a multimodal mixture-of-experts transformer with approximately 26 billion physical parameters, of which 3 billion are active for each token. It accepts text and images and supports a context size of one million tokens. The unusual design feature is looping: twelve middle decoder layers run three times, giving 48 logical layer visits from 24 physical layers while reusing the same weights.

Hugging Face says the release is supported in Transformers, Llama App through llama.cpp, MLX, and SGLang. It also reports four-bit and eight-bit quantized releases for local use. A separate research variant accepts one, two, or three passes. Hugging Face says fewer passes make inference substantially faster, but may reduce output quality; it tested the variant without evaluating it in depth.

On performance, the publisher says Inkling 2 Mini leads 32 of 48 listed benchmarks against four named models, including coding, agentic search, vision, reasoning, and safety evaluations. It leads FrontierCode version 2.0, ties one terminal benchmark, and trails another. These are publisher-reported results. Hugging Face’s separate “vibe tests” found the model at least comparable with Qwen 3.8 27B, but the publisher explicitly says more validation is needed.

For a reproducible classroom or research workflow, teams could compare fixed and variable-pass inference using identical prompts and record output quality, latency, and hardware requirements. The documented limitation is that the publisher does not provide enough validation detail here to establish that the model performs consistently better across real-world local workloads.

## 5. AI-Assisted Review Maps Code Sharing Gaps

MIT News reported that an MIT and Dana-Farber team used large language models to examine nearly 4,000 clinical prediction studies and hundreds of linked code repositories. The review found that code sharing was uncommon, and publicly available code often lacked the documentation and supporting information needed for reuse.

<p class="small text-muted">Technology &amp; research · MIT News · 2026-11-19 · <a href="https://news.mit.edu/2026/ai-helps-researchers-investigate-how-medical-studies-share-code-1119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Primary source</a></p>

<details><summary>Read the full narration</summary>

MIT News reported on November 19, 2026, that researchers led by MIT and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute used large language models to examine nearly 4,000 clinical prediction studies and hundreds of linked code repositories. The reported findings were published in Nature Medicine. The team used the models to screen papers, locate statements about code availability, and extract repository links. Software then retrieved those repositories for assessment of factors including documentation, software requirements, and terms of reuse. Human reviewers independently checked 500 papers and a separate repository sample, resolving disagreements before comparing their judgments with automated results. MIT News said agreement was stronger for concrete features, such as the presence of a license file, than for evaluating whether documentation was adequate. Because classification errors remain possible, the authors advised readers to focus on overall patterns rather than treating every count as exact. The review found that only about one in eight studies reported sharing code, while repository quality varied widely. Across journals, the reported proportion ranged from zero to more than 70 percent. A practical teaching point, separate from the reported findings, is to show students why a repository link alone is not enough: reliable reuse may require clear instructions, documented software requirements, and explicit reuse terms.

</details>

## 3. IBM’s Granite LLMON preview targets structured model instructions

Hugging Face published an IBM Granite article on November 12, 2026, describing LLMON, an LLM-native markup language now included in two Granite preview models. IBM says LLMON marks instructions, data, and output requirements with meaningful tags and reserved delimiters, giving each component an addressable identifier. Unlike JSON, whose punctuation is also ordinary language-model input, the format separates structural tokens from content and states nested structure locally. IBM released Granite 4.1 3B and 8B experimental previews, plus a repository containing tools, examples, and lossless converters between JSON and LLMON. The article says these models were trained from Granite base checkpoints, not the released instruction-tuned versions, with LLMON behavior learned during post-training. In IBM’s OPI benchmark evaluation, the previews avoided 47% more attacks than the best frontier model tested and 95% more than the best open-source model tested. IBM also reports similar trends on ASB and StruQ, and says general benchmark performance was on par with Granite 4.1 Instruct models. These are publisher-reported results, not independent verification. The documented limitation is scope: the benchmarks cover static indirect injections, while adaptive attacks unfolding over many chat turns remained an active development area. IBM also describes the learned privilege behavior as not an enforced guarantee. The release is explicitly experimental and not for production. For teaching, a reproducible exercise could compare the same instruction with JSON and LLMON, while documenting sample size and results.

## 4. Researchers Report Low-Temperature Densification of Ceramic Films

ScienceNet News reports that a Chinese Academy of Sciences team developed a liquid-phase strategy for growing dense ceramic films at 800 °C and atmospheric pressure. The article says the method produced a roughly 10-micrometre BZY electrolyte film with uniform composition and demonstrated large-area preparation.

<details><summary>Read the full narration</summary>

ScienceNet News reported on 12 November 2026 that researchers from the Chinese Academy of Sciences’ Dalian Institute of Chemical Physics proposed a liquid-phase growth strategy for ceramic films. The approach guides oriented crystal growth in a liquid medium and, according to the article, breaks the traditional dependence on high-temperature sintering from a mechanistic standpoint.

The team used yttrium-doped barium zirconate, a proton conductor with a melting point of about 2700 °C, as its model material. It employed molten carbonate as the liquid reaction environment and used a seed layer to direct nucleation and crystal growth. At 800 °C and atmospheric pressure, the researchers prepared a dense, compositionally uniform BZY electrolyte film about 10 micrometres thick.

ScienceNet News said the strategy can suppress elemental segregation, secondary-phase formation and interfacial elemental interdiffusion relative to conventional high-temperature sintering. In-situ characterization captured a sequence involving carbonate melting, precursor dissolution, reaction nucleation, crystal growth and continuous film formation. The team’s single-cell tests showed good electrochemical performance, although the article did not provide numerical performance data.

The researchers also demonstrated large-area ceramic-film preparation and said the method was applicable to metal substrates and other ceramic materials. The report described these as research results and a possible route for low-temperature manufacturing; it did not establish industrial-scale readiness or independently verify broader performance claims. The related paper was published in Nature Synthesis.

## 5. JWST detects a faint trihydrogen signal

ScienceAlert reports that astronomers using the James Webb Space Telescope detected previously unseen rotational emission from the trihydrogen cation in two Herbig-Haro objects. The researchers identified 26 spectral lines, but shock-wave models did not fully explain the signal’s brightness.

<p class="small text-muted">Technology &amp; research · ScienceAlert · 2026-11-12 · <a href="https://www.sciencealert.com/jwst-detects-a-forbidden-signal-never-before-seen-in-the-universe-not-even-in-a-lab" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Original report</a></p>

<details><summary>Read the full narration</summary>

ScienceAlert reported on 12 November 2026 that a team led by David Neufeld of Johns Hopkins University used the James Webb Space Telescope to detect previously unobserved rotational emission from the trihydrogen cation in two Herbig-Haro objects, HH 168 and HH 211. The trihydrogen cation has three hydrogen nuclei and two electrons. Its symmetrical triangular shape normally prevents ordinary rotational radiation, although physicists Fu-Shih Pan and Takeshi Oka predicted in 1986 that rotation could create a tiny electric imbalance and produce extremely weak spectral lines. ScienceAlert said laboratory searches had failed to detect that predicted signal for four decades. In HH 168, the team identified 26 distinct lines with high statistical confidence; the strongest was reported at 72 sigma. The emission closely tracked ordinary molecular hydrogen, suggesting a shared response to the shocked gas. However, the models did not fully explain the observation. One reproduced ordinary molecular hydrogen but underestimated trihydrogen by a factor of five or six; another matched trihydrogen better but used conditions beyond its validated range. The signal was also found in archival observations of HH 211. The research was accepted by The Astrophysical Journal Letters and available on arXiv. The source presents the detection as firm, while the physical explanation for the unexpectedly bright emission remains unresolved.

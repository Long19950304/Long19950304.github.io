1. Traditional Industries Add Digital Tools

Xinhua Finance reports that the first Historical Classics Industry Expo in Hangzhou showcased how traditional manufacturers are using AI, standardized processes, and modern equipment. Customs data cited for Longquan sword-related products also show year-on-year export growth during January–September 2026.

China economy · 新华社财经 · 2026-11-21 · Original report

Read the full narration Xinhua Finance reported on November 21, 2026, that the first Historical Classics Industry Expo opened in Hangzhou on November 20. The event covered about 30,000 square metres and brought together more than 400 key enterprises and well-known regional brands across silk, tea, porcelain, brewing, crafts, and other sectors. Digital tools were prominent. According to Wanshili Silk chairman Li Jianhua, the company’s AI design model uses a database and graphic algorithms to help designers optimise designs and shorten design cycles. He also said its in-house green printing and dyeing equipment compresses a traditional production flow occupying roughly 5,000 square metres into an integrated machine of about 50 square metres, with nearly zero wastewater discharge. These are company statements reported by Xinhua, not independently verified performance findings. Standardisation was another focus. At the China Tea Longguan booth, staff described dividing tea production into 26 steps, identifying 12 quality-control points, and weighting five factors: colour, aroma, taste, shape, and leaf bottom. The stated goal was a more stable taste. Xinhua also cited Lishui Customs data for January through September 2026: Longquan sword-related exports reached RMB 56.16 million, up 44.7 percent year on year. Customs attributed the growth to anime and game props priced at tens or hundreds of US dollars rather than high-end collector swords. The reported growth reflects exports during that nine-month period; it does not establish a full-year result.

2. U.S. Airlines Shift International First Class to Business

CNBC Business reports that American Airlines has ended international first-class service as U.S. carriers expand larger business-class cabins. The article covers reported cabin changes, pricing examples and continued segmentation of premium travel.

World economy · CNBC Business · 2026-11-21 · Original report

Read the full narration CNBC Business reported on November 21, 2026, that American Airlines’ final flight with its international first-class section marked the end of that service among U.S. carriers. According to CNBC, American’s Boeing 777-300ER cabins will hold 70 business-class suites, while the previous configuration had eight first-class suites and 52 business-class pods. The article says Delta Air Lines and United Airlines had already replaced international, long-haul first class with upgraded business-class products. American also plans to keep a separate lie-flat first class on its Airbus A321T subfleet for domestic cross-country flights until early 2027, after which that offering will end. CNBC reported one Los Angeles-to-London fare on American’s Boeing 777-300ER at $12,994 for business class and $15,669 for first class, both for one-way tickets. The report also described larger suites, improved bedding, device-charging pads and premium-economy products. It noted that basic and more elaborate business-class options are increasing, while first class continues internationally at carriers including Singapore Airlines, Lufthansa, British Airways and Air France. A documented limitation is that CNBC’s fare examples concern specific flights and are not presented as representative prices across routes or dates.

3. Customized Light for Plant Factories

ScienceNet News reports that a Chinese research team developed a plant-factory system that adjusts lighting for different crops and feeds measurements back into a closed loop. The team also combines optical filtering with photovoltaic generation and is gathering crop-growth data to iteratively optimize lighting recipes.

Technology & research · 科学网新闻 · 2026-11-21 · Original report

Read the full narration ScienceNet News, citing China News Service reporter Zhang Jun, described a research team at the Advanced Technology Institute of the University of Science and Technology of China that has studied light and crops for more than ten years. Its plant-factory system adjusts lamp intensity, spectrum, and photoperiod for different plants. A multichannel light quantum sensor measures photon numbers in individual wavelength bands, and the readings return to the control system for closed-loop adjustment, according to the report. The team also developed a polymer optical membrane. It transmits useful red and blue light to plants while reflecting other wavelengths toward photovoltaic panels for electricity generation. The report says the membrane blocks most heat, leaving plant surfaces somewhat cooler and reducing water evaporation. These are the research team’s described characteristics, rather than independently verified performance measurements. For tomatoes, the team says natural red-to-blue light is about four to five, while its factory setting is nine to one. The report attributes faster lycopene production and better taste to that setting. Crop-growth data collected under different recipes are also used to train a plant-growth model and iteratively refine the recipes. The article says container factories have entered deserts, islands, and snowy highlands and have traveled aboard cargo ships, with products sold in Malaysia, Singapore, and other markets. Its forward-looking target is lower energy use and lower cost, but no timetable or quantified reduction was provided.

4. Inkling 2 Mini Targets Local AI

Hugging Face says Thinking Machines Lab’s Inkling 2 Mini is a multimodal, looped mixture-of-experts model designed for local AI setups. The publisher reports broad tooling support, but explicitly says its initial comparison is based on informal tests and needs more validation.

Technology & research · Hugging Face · 2026-11-20 · Primary source

Read the full narration Hugging Face announced Inkling 2 Mini on November 20, 2026, describing it as a new model from Thinking Machines Lab designed for local AI setups. According to the publisher, it is a multimodal mixture-of-experts transformer with approximately 26 billion physical parameters, of which 3 billion are active for each token. It accepts text and images and supports a context size of one million tokens. The unusual design feature is looping: twelve middle decoder layers run three times, giving 48 logical layer visits from 24 physical layers while reusing the same weights. Hugging Face says the release is supported in Transformers, Llama App through llama.cpp, MLX, and SGLang. It also reports four-bit and eight-bit quantized releases for local use. A separate research variant accepts one, two, or three passes. Hugging Face says fewer passes make inference substantially faster, but may reduce output quality; it tested the variant without evaluating it in depth. On performance, the publisher says Inkling 2 Mini leads 32 of 48 listed benchmarks against four named models, including coding, agentic search, vision, reasoning, and safety evaluations. It leads FrontierCode version 2.0, ties one terminal benchmark, and trails another. These are publisher-reported results. Hugging Face’s separate “vibe tests” found the model at least comparable with Qwen 3.8 27B, but the publisher explicitly says more validation is needed. For a reproducible classroom or research workflow, teams could compare fixed and variable-pass inference using identical prompts and record output quality, latency, and hardware requirements. The documented limitation is that the publisher does not provide enough validation detail here to establish that the model performs consistently better across real-world local workloads.

5. AI-Assisted Review Maps Code Sharing Gaps

MIT News reported that an MIT and Dana-Farber team used large language models to examine nearly 4,000 clinical prediction studies and hundreds of linked code repositories. The review found that code sharing was uncommon, and publicly available code often lacked the documentation and supporting information needed for reuse.

Technology & research · MIT News · 2026-11-19 · Primary source

Read the full narration MIT News reported on November 19, 2026, that researchers led by MIT and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute used large language models to examine nearly 4,000 clinical prediction studies and hundreds of linked code repositories. The reported findings were published in Nature Medicine. The team used the models to screen papers, locate statements about code availability, and extract repository links. Software then retrieved those repositories for assessment of factors including documentation, software requirements, and terms of reuse. Human reviewers independently checked 500 papers and a separate repository sample, resolving disagreements before comparing their judgments with automated results. MIT News said agreement was stronger for concrete features, such as the presence of a license file, than for evaluating whether documentation was adequate. Because classification errors remain possible, the authors advised readers to focus on overall patterns rather than treating every count as exact. The review found that only about one in eight studies reported sharing code, while repository quality varied widely. Across journals, the reported proportion ranged from zero to more than 70 percent. A practical teaching point, separate from the reported findings, is to show students why a repository link alone is not enough: reliable reuse may require clear instructions, documented software requirements, and explicit reuse terms.
